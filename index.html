Read before you mix

Rain water: catch it in a clean plastic or glass container after the first 15 minutes of rain. Avoid old painted roofs. Strain through clean cloth.

Keep A and B apart: calcium mixed with phosphate and sulphate goes cloudy. Add the stocks to the water one at a time, stirring.

Stocks: keep in the fridge. If crystals form, warm in hot water and shake until clear. Filter if cloudy. Throw away if mouldy.

Scale: weigh small amounts on a 0.01 g jewellery scale.

pH: raise with 1M NaOH (pH tab), lower with a drop of white vinegar. Aim for 5.7 to 5.8, then add agar.

Cooking: pressure cooker at full pressure for about 20 minutes. Jars 30 to 40 ml.

Honest warning: without ammonium nitrate this medium has less nitrogen than true MS. Pineapple copes, but test it side by side with a bought MS batch if you can.

Hormones: buy BAP and NAA from a lab supplier. Garden rooting powders are not suitable.

From culture jar to resilient plant

Plantlets leaving sterile culture have delicate leaves, limited control of water loss and roots adapted to a protected environment. Acclimatization is a gradual transition—not a single transplant.

Use ranges as starting points, not universal recipes. Species, cultivar, vessel ventilation, root quality and local climate change the ideal settings. Record conditions and adjust gradually; avoid sudden jumps in light, humidity or temperature.

Step-by-step hardening pathway

🧪1 · Check before removing

Choose firm, green plantlets with active growth and usable roots. Remove weak, necrotic or contaminated cultures. If plants came from virus-recovery work, keep them identified and separate until diagnostic clearance is documented.

🚿2 · Clean roots gently

Remove agar from roots with clean, lukewarm water; leftover medium can encourage microbial growth. Handle roots by the root ball, not by pulling the stem. Avoid damaging fine roots.

🌫️3 · Start humid, not stagnant

Pot into a clean, airy, well-drained substrate. Use a clear humidity cover or misting setup if needed, but provide some air exchange. Ventilate more each day as the plant holds turgor.

🌤️4 · Increase light gradually

Begin in bright, indirect or filtered light. Avoid direct midday sun at first. Increase light in small steps over days to weeks, watching for bleaching, leaf scorch or wilting.

💧5 · Keep roots moist and aerated

Keep the substrate evenly moist, never continuously saturated. Empty standing water from trays. Reduce misting as roots and new leaves become functional.

🌬️6 · Build airflow and strength

Increase ventilation gradually and avoid cold or hot drafts. Once plants remain firm without a cover, move them to the nursery conditions they will actually experience.

🪴7 · Feed only when ready

Do not apply strong fertilizer to newly transferred plantlets. Wait for signs of active growth and functioning roots, then begin with a dilute, crop-appropriate nutrient program.

📝8 · Track each batch

Label species, cultivar, batch, transfer date and treatment. Record survival, new-leaf growth, root condition, losses and changes to humidity/light so the next batch improves.

Adjust the approach to the crop

🍍 Pineapple

Use a very free-draining medium and warm conditions. Avoid water collecting in the crown or around the base; increase light gradually after establishment.

🍎 Apple & temperate fruits

Protect tender plantlets from heat spikes and strong direct sun. Maintain even moisture and adjust hardening to the intended nursery climate and cultivar.

🍇 Grapevine

Prioritize root-zone aeration, gentle light increases and clean handling. Keep cultivar and rootstock identity separate where grafting is planned.

🌴 Date palm & other slow starters

Avoid drying the root zone, but do not leave containers waterlogged. Give extra time to establish before exposing plants to strong sun or dry wind.

Beginner bottlenecks & corrections

Leaves wilt soon after transfer

Humidity may have dropped too quickly or roots may be damaged. Restore a protected, ventilated environment, check root health and restart a slower hardening schedule.

Fuzzy growth or stem-base rot

Reduce persistent leaf wetness, improve airflow, remove affected material using clean tools and review substrate drainage and sanitation.

Leaves bleach or scorch

Move to filtered light and increase exposure more gradually. Check for heat buildup under clear covers.

Plantlets stop growing

Check roots, temperature, moisture, substrate pH/salts and whether fertilizer was added too early. Change one factor at a time and record the result.

Ready to leave the hardening chamber? A plantlet should remain firm under normal nursery humidity, show new growth, and have roots that hold the substrate together. Hardened does not mean virus-free: keep plant-health testing and batch traceability separate from acclimatization records.

Clean Plantlet Recovery Lab

A practical, evidence-led map for recovering valuable fruit varieties when a virus is suspected. The goal is not merely to grow a new shoot—it is to produce a plant that tests negative for the target pathogen and stays protected from reinfection.

🧪 Diagnosis first🌱 Meristem-based recovery🔬 Re-test before release🛡️ Protect clean stock
Important: leaf symptoms alone cannot confirm a virus, and a healthy-looking plant is not proof of virus freedom. Some infections are latent. Use a plant diagnostic laboratory for the target-virus test; don't label or distribute recovered plants as virus-free until testing supports that claim.
1. Suspect & isolate

Mottling, rings, distortion or stunting are clues—not a diagnosis.

2. Recover the apex

Very small shoot-tip/meristem tissue may escape some viruses; success depends on crop and pathogen.

3. Regenerate & root

Establish candidate shoots aseptically, multiply carefully, then root using a crop-specific protocol.

4. Index & protect

Test recovered candidates and later stock plants; keep them separated from infected plants and vectors.

Recovery pathway, from the beginning

1Confirm the problem and quarantine the source

Do first: label the suspect plant, keep it separate, avoid taking cuttings from it for ordinary multiplication, and disinfect tools between plants. Record crop, cultivar, symptoms, location and when symptoms appeared.

  • Submit suitable samples to a plant diagnostic laboratory for virus-specific testing (for example ELISA or PCR/RT-PCR, depending on the virus).
  • Rule out look-alikes such as nutrient disorders, mites, herbicide injury and fungal or bacterial disease.
  • Do not compost or distribute suspect propagation material; follow local plant-health advice for disposal and vector control.
2Choose the sanitation method for that crop and virus

Core method: shoot-tip or apical-meristem culture uses a small growing point where some viruses occur at lower levels. Smaller meristems may improve elimination, but are harder to dissect and regenerate—especially in woody fruit crops.

Possible specialist combinations: thermotherapy before meristem culture, micrografting of tiny shoot tips, or other validated treatments. The best combination depends on the host cultivar, virus/viroid, infection level and survival of the tiny explant.

Do not copy a temperature/time recipe across every fruit. Published treatment ranges are crop- and pathogen-specific; use a validated protocol or plant-biotechnology lab. Cryotherapy requires specialist equipment and is not a beginner home-lab step.

3Establish the tiny explant aseptically

Work in a properly equipped sterile tissue-culture facility. Use clean instruments, sterile vessels and a medium validated for the crop. Meristem excision is delicate; contamination or damage can destroy the explant before it grows.

  • Keep each candidate line identifiable and separate; record the source plant, treatment, date and vessel ID.
  • Discard contaminated cultures safely according to facility rules; don't mix them back into clean lines.
  • Allow slow-growing candidates time appropriate to the crop—do not select only the fastest-growing shoots if that compromises traceability or quality.
4Multiply, root and acclimatize as candidate lines

Once a candidate shoot establishes, multiply it using a crop-specific medium and passage schedule, then induce roots or use micrografting where the crop protocol requires it. Avoid excessive subculturing and monitor for abnormal growth or off-types.

  • Rooted plantlets are usually moved gradually from high humidity to nursery conditions: begin protected, increase ventilation and light stepwise, and avoid sudden drying.
  • Keep candidate lines physically separated from untested material during hardening.
  • Good growth, normal leaves and successful rooting show plantlet quality—not virus freedom.
5Test again before calling it virus-free

Retest regenerated plants for the original target virus (and any additional pathogens required by the crop certification scheme). Testing may need to be repeated after growth because very low virus levels can escape detection in a single sample.

  • Keep the laboratory report linked to the clone or batch ID.
  • If a candidate tests positive or the result is inconclusive, keep it out of clean stock and seek advice on repeating or changing the sanitation method.
  • Only describe a line as virus-free/virus-negative for the tested targets and test conditions—not universally free of every pathogen.
6Build and maintain a protected clean mother block

Maintain confirmed negative stock in a clean, monitored area away from infected plants. Control insect vectors where relevant, use clean tools and containers, inspect routinely, and retest on a schedule appropriate to the crop and certification scheme.

Track each multiplication batch back to the tested mother line. A virus-free plant can become reinfected after release if exposed to vectors or infected planting material.

Beginner failure points to avoid

⚠️ “It looks healthy”Appearance alone is not a virus test. Use diagnostic testing before and after recovery.
⚠️ Explant too largeLarger shoot tips may regenerate more easily but can retain virus. Use a validated size and technique for that crop.
⚠️ ContaminationSeparate lines, use proper sterile technique, and keep contaminated vessels out of clean culture areas.
⚠️ ReinfectionKeep tested stock away from infected plants and manage vectors; test stock again as required.
